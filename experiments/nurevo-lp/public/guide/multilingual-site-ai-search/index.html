AI search for multilingual sites: how agencies design and check each language version

To have AI and search engines read a multilingual site correctly, check four things when you build it and every month after: one language per page, a separate URL for each language, hreflang linking every language version both ways, and the same facts - opening hours, prices, address - in every language. Google says it decides a page's language from its visible content, not from the lang attribute or hreflang. ChatGPT and Perplexity have published nothing on how they treat hreflang, and in one set of tests they often cited the English version, so the facts must be right in whichever language version gets cited.

Written 24 November 2026 (JST). For agencies and freelancers who build or maintain multilingual sites: hotels, restaurants and attractions serving overseas visitors, manufacturers with foreign buyers, clinics that see international patients. Google's guidance is as published in Search Central when this was written.

AI answers change by day, by wording and by the user's language settings. These steps set up the conditions for being read correctly; they do not guarantee an appearance in AI answers or any ranking.

What is known, and what is not

Advice on multilingual AI optimisation often mixes official search-engine guidance with guesses about AI. Separating them avoids misleading clients.

StatementType and source
Google decides a page's language from its visible content, not the lang attribute or the URLGoogle guidance (Managing multi-regional and multilingual sites)
Google does not use hreflang or the lang attribute to detect language; hreflang tells Google about the language versions of the same contentGoogle guidance (Tell Google about localized versions)
If two pages do not both point to each other, the tags are ignoredGoogle guidance (Tell Google about localized versions)
Content translated by AI is not spam in itself, but mass-producing low-value pages is covered by the spam policy, whose examples include "automated transformations" such as translatingGoogle guidance (Spam policies; Google's comment via Search Engine Roundtable and GSQi, June 2025)
At Bing, hreflang is a far weaker signal than content-languageA Bing engineer's remark (Search Engine Roundtable, September 2020; old, may have changed)
Whether ChatGPT, Perplexity or Claude read hreflang, and which language version they citeNo official documentation found; unknown

One useful set of observations: SEO consultant Glenn Gabe asked several AI tools questions in French, Italian and Spanish about pages that exist in many languages (Google documentation, a Cloudflare post) and recorded the language of the cited URL (GSQi, December 2025). Google's AI Mode and AI Overviews and Copilot mostly cited the version in the question's language; ChatGPT and Claude cited the US English version in all three examples; Perplexity got the language right in one. With three examples, and the author calling it "not a perfect science", treat this as a hint, not a rule.

So rather than trying to make AI pick each language version, a realistic agency goal is a site where whichever language version is cited has correct facts and links to the other languages.

Design decisions: URL structure and language switching

Google recommends a separate URL for each language and advises against switching the page language with cookies or browser settings. The options:

Based on the table in Google's guide to multi-regional sites, with our notes for agencies.
StructureWhen it fits, and cautions
Subdirectory (example.jp/en/)One domain to manage and easy with WordPress multilingual plugins. The first option to consider for small and mid-sized sites
Subdomain (en.example.jp)When each language runs on a different server or system. The target country is not obvious from the URL
Country domain (example.de)When each country has its own company or stock. Costly to run, and each domain has its own rules
URL parameter (?lang=en)Google does not recommend it. Do not use it on new sites

Avoid automatic switching - redirecting visitors to another language based on IP address or browser language - and put links to the other languages on every page. Google says Googlebot usually crawls from the US without sending Accept-Language, so automatic redirects can stop some language versions being read. It is safest to assume AI crawlers also arrive from fixed locations without a language preference.

Write one language per page and avoid side-by-side original and translation. Pages where only the navigation and footer are translated while the main text stays in Japanese tend to be treated as duplicates by Google; it is safer not to publish an untranslated page as a language version.

Facts to keep identical, and what to localise

An AI may cite the English page and answer in Japanese, or the other way round. If languages are updated at different times, old hours or prices get quoted in another language. Everything marked "keep identical" below must have the same value in every language.

ItemKeep identical / localise
Opening hours, closing days, temporary closuresKeep identical. Japanese often gets updated while other languages lag, so put every language into the update routine
Prices, tax-inclusive or not, currencyKeep the amount identical. Show yen prices in yen in every language and label any foreign-currency figure as approximate
Phone numberKeep identical, and add the +81 form for callers from abroad
Address and mapThe same place, written in the order readers of that language expect
Business nameDecide one official form and add a romanised or translated name in other languages, e.g. Sakura Soba (さくら蕎麦)
How to book or contactKeep identical. If a channel cannot handle a language, say so
Languages spoken, payment methods, allergen informationAdd what visitors in that language need, and put the facts on the Japanese page too
Headings and wordingLocalise: write what people in that language actually search for, not a literal translation

Writing hreflang, and common mistakes

hreflang can go in HTML link elements, HTTP headers or the sitemap; Google treats the three as equivalent. On WordPress, multilingual plugins usually print link elements in the head. An example:

<!-- Example: the access page in Japanese, English, Traditional Chinese, Korean.
     Put the same five lines on all four pages (each page lists itself too) -->
<link rel="alternate" hreflang="ja" href="https://example.jp/ja/access/">
<link rel="alternate" hreflang="en" href="https://example.jp/en/access/">
<link rel="alternate" hreflang="zh-Hant" href="https://example.jp/zh-hant/access/">
<link rel="alternate" hreflang="ko" href="https://example.jp/ko/access/">
<link rel="alternate" hreflang="x-default" href="https://example.jp/en/access/">
Common mistakeCorrect form
Tags on the Japanese page only, none on the English pageEvery language version lists all versions including itself (tags that do not point both ways are ignored)
Relative paths (/en/access/)Full URLs starting with https
Wrong codes such as jp, en-UK or es-419Language in ISO 639-1 (ja, en), region in ISO 3166-1 (en-GB). A country code alone is not valid. Traditional Chinese: zh-Hant or zh-TW
Listing URLs that redirect or are noindexList only pages that load directly and may appear in search
Printing every language on pages that are not translatedList only translated versions, and add a line when a translation is added

x-default is the page for people whose language matches none of the versions - usually a language selector or the English version.

This site, nurevo.jp, publishes guides in 41 languages. Articles that exist only in Japanese and English list only Japanese, English (with its regional variants) and x-default in hreflang, and a language is added when its translation is published, so no tag ever points to a page that does not exist.

A script to check that versions point to each other

Even when a plugin prints hreflang, reciprocity breaks when some pages lack a translation, a URL changes or a redirect is added. This script opens every language version listed on one page, checks that each lists itself and the starting page, and shows the html lang attribute. It needs only Python 3's standard library.

# Usage: python3 check_hreflang.py https://example.jp/en/access/
# Opens every language version listed in the page's hreflang and checks that
# each one lists itself and the page you started from.
# Result: OK / NOT_RECIPROCAL = the pair does not point both ways (Google ignores it)
#         REDIRECTED = the listed URL redirects / NOT_ABSOLUTE = not a full URL
#         The html_lang column shows whether <html lang> matches the language
import re, sys, urllib.request

def fetch(url):
    req = urllib.request.Request(url, headers={"User-Agent": "Mozilla/5.0 (hreflang-check)"})
    with urllib.request.urlopen(req, timeout=20) as res:
        return res.geturl(), res.status, res.read().decode("utf-8", "replace")

def alternates(html):
    found = {}
    for tag in re.findall(r"<link\b[^>]*>", html, re.I):
        if not re.search(r'rel=["\']?alternate', tag, re.I):
            continue
        lang = re.search(r'hreflang=["\']([^"\']+)', tag, re.I)
        href = re.search(r'href=["\']([^"\']+)', tag, re.I)
        if lang and href:
            found[lang.group(1).lower()] = href.group(1)
    return found

def html_lang(html):
    m = re.search(r"<html\b[^>]*\blang=[\"']([^\"']+)", html, re.I)
    return m.group(1) if m else ""

start = sys.argv[1]
final, status, html = fetch(start)
base = alternates(html)
if not base:
    sys.exit(f"{start}: no hreflang links found")
print("hreflang\turl\tstatus\thtml_lang\tlists_itself\tlists_start\tresult")
for lang, url in sorted(base.items()):
    if not url.startswith(("http://", "https://")):
        print(f"{lang}\t{url}\t\t\t\t\tNOT_ABSOLUTE")
        continue
    try:
        got, code, page = fetch(url)
    except Exception as e:
        print(f"{lang}\t{url}\tERROR {e}\t\t\t\tFETCH_ERROR")
        continue
    alt = alternates(page)
    self_ok = url in alt.values()
    back_ok = final in alt.values() or start in alt.values()
    result = "OK"
    if got != url:
        result = "REDIRECTED"
    elif not (self_ok and back_ok):
        result = "NOT_RECIPROCAL"
    print(f"{lang}\t{url}\t{code}\t{html_lang(page)}\t{self_ok}\t{back_ok}\t{result}")

Before launch, run it on pages full of facts: home, access, prices, FAQ. In maintenance, run it on a few pages each month. On NOT_RECIPROCAL or REDIRECTED, check the page's translation link in the plugin and its URL. Google does not use the lang attribute to detect language, but screen readers and other tools do, so keep it correct for each language.

Structured data and llms.txt: what changes per language

Business structured data (LocalBusiness or Organization) describes the same single business on every language version. So it does not look like a different business per language, we suggest the following (this is our recommendation, not a Google rule):

  1. Use the same @id for the business node in every language (e.g. https://example.jp/#business), with the same phone number, address and opening hours.
  2. Put the name readers of that page understand in name, and the official Japanese form in alternateName (or the other way round, but the same rule in every language).
  3. Set inLanguage on the page node (WebPage etc.) to that page's language: ja, en, zh-Hant and so on.
  4. Write FAQPage questions and answers in the language visible on the page. Do not put questions in structured data in a language the page does not show.
  5. llms.txt usually sits once at the site root. Write it in the main language and link to the home page of each other language so readers can see which language entrances exist.

WordPress steps (Polylang, WPML and Nurevo AEO)

The two main WordPress multilingual plugins, Polylang and WPML, both print hreflang automatically (Polylang documentation, WPML documentation). Differences and where to check:

  1. Polylang converts the WordPress locale (en_US) into hreflang form (en-US). By default it prints x-default on the home page only; to add it elsewhere, its documentation shows a filter (pll_rel_hreflang_attributes).
  2. WPML lets you change each language's hreflang code (for example to add a region) in its language settings. WPML says it cannot warn you about an invalid format, so check the codes against the table above.
  3. Make sure no SEO plugin or theme prints a second set of hreflang tags: view the page source and look for exactly one set.
  4. On each language version, use View Source to check the html lang attribute and hreflang, then run the script above.
  5. The free plugin Nurevo AEO sets inLanguage in the page's structured data from the WordPress language setting (get_bloginfo('language')). It has no Polylang- or WPML-specific code, so check that inLanguage matches the language on each version. Business details are one set of settings, so every language shows the same facts. llms.txt is built once, in the site's language.
  6. Before launch, compare opening hours, prices and phone numbers on the key pages of every language with the Japanese pages.

Checking each language

  1. Write a question list per language, e.g. "soba near X station English menu" in English, Chinese and Korean - phrased the way people in that language would ask, not translated word for word.
  2. Ask with the AI tool's language setting (or the account's language) changed, and record the answer's language, the cited URL's language and whether the facts are right.
  3. In Search Console's performance report, filter pages by directory such as /en/ and look at impressions by country.
  4. In your analytics, look at visits from AI services per language directory.
  5. When a wrong fact appears, check the cited page's language and last update, and fix that language version.

AI answers change every time, so compare monthly records rather than one result. The full method is in how to measure AI visibility.

  2. No automatic redirects by IP or browser language; every page links to the other languages

FAQ

If I add hreflang, will ChatGPT and Perplexity cite each language version?

Unknown. We found no official statement that ChatGPT, Perplexity or Claude use hreflang, and in published tests they often cited the English version. Make sure the facts are correct whichever language version gets cited.

Can I build the language versions with machine translation?

Google does not treat AI-translated content as spam in itself, but mass-producing low-value pages is covered by its spam policy. If you use machine translation, have someone who knows the language check facts and wording before publishing.

Do I have to translate every page?

No. Start with what visitors in that language need: home, access, prices, booking and FAQ. Do not publish untranslated pages as language versions, and leave them out of hreflang.

Is it wrong to switch to English automatically based on the browser language?

Google recommends avoiding automatic redirects and linking to the other languages instead. Crawlers often arrive without a language preference, so redirects can stop some versions being read.

Should structured data use the Japanese or the English business name?

We suggest the name readers of that page understand in name and the other form in alternateName. Keep the business node's @id, phone number, address and hours the same in every language.

Do I need both Simplified and Traditional Chinese?

It depends on the client's visitors. Many from Taiwan or Hong Kong call for Traditional (zh-Hant or zh-TW), many from mainland China for Simplified (zh-Hans or zh-CN). If you make both, give each its own URL and link them with hreflang.

Will a multilingual site appear in AI answers for overseas users?

That cannot be guaranteed. AI answers vary by day, wording and the user's language. A well-built multilingual site makes sure that when it does appear, the facts are right and people can reach the page in their language.
