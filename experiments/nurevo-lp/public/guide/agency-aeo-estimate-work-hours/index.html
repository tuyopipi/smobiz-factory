How to estimate AI search (AEO/GEO) work: hours per task, the estimate sheet and what goes on the quote

Estimate AI search work (AEO, GEO, LLMO) by building it up, not by working back from market ranges. Split the work into four phases (audit, setup, monthly upkeep, measurement and reporting), turn whatever drives the hours for each task into a quantity, and multiply your own measured time per unit by your hourly rate. Because no one can guarantee that an AI will mention a business, price the work and the deliverables, not the outcome, and write the scope, the exclusions and how changes are handled on the quote.

Written November 28, 2026 (JST). We read the published pricing guides listed below on the same day.

This guide is for web agencies, SEO consultants and freelancers who want to sell AI search work, or who have been asked for a quote and are not sure how to price it. What a retainer should cover is in turning AI search into a monthly service, and the pre-sale audit and proposal are in the first audit and proposal. This page covers only how to get to a number. It is not legal or tax advice.

What the top-ranking pricing guides leave out

On November 28, 2026 we read seven English and five Japanese pages that rank for searches like "AEO pricing", "GEO cost" and "how to price AEO services". They are good at budget tiers and at red flags for buyers. But almost all of them are written for the buyer. From the agency's side, these were missing:

  1. For each task, what makes it take longer (pages, locations, FAQ items, the SEO plugin already on the site)
  2. How to measure your own hours and turn them into unit rates, with a sheet you can reuse
  3. The assumptions to put on the quote: exclusions, change handling, and wording that does not promise results
  4. How to cut hours on WordPress

The sections below fill those gaps.

The ranges are wide because the bundles differ. A program that includes content production and digital PR is a different job from tightening the structured data, llms.txt, FAQ and listings for a local business. Pricing from your own tasks is also easier to explain to the client than "that is the market rate".

Measure your own hours on the first three clients

Build the hour figures from your own logs, not from someone else's article. The published hour ranges assume different work: Stackmatix's 10–20 hour audit and 5–10 hours of monthly implementation describe consulting engagements, and a Japanese vendor's estimate of 63–120 hours a month for in-house work includes 20–40 hours of content production. Neither describes the technical setup of a small business site.

Sources: Stackmatix; Kadai Kaiketsu Platform (the vendor's own assumption, in Japanese).

  1. Give each task in the table a short code (Audit = A, Fact sheet = F, Schema = S, llms.txt = L, FAQ = Q, Crawlers = C, Listings = P, Measurement = M, Reporting = R)
  2. On the first three clients, log start and end times per task. Log time spent waiting for the client separately as "waiting"
  3. Log the quantity alongside the time (prompts, locations, page templates)
  4. After three clients, work out the time per unit for each task and use the median, so one unusual job does not skew it
  5. Review every three months. If tooling or practice cuts the time, decide whether to lower the price or add work at the same price

Set the hourly rate from your own costs, not from the market. Divide your yearly fixed costs (salaries, rent, tools) by the billable hours you actually sell in a year to get the floor, then add your margin.

Total hours x hourly rate + tools (at cost) + contingency (X% of total) = quote
* Time per unit = the median of what you logged on your first three clients.

Contingency covers what you cannot predict: slow reviews, listings nobody can log in to. Set the percentage from the gap between estimated and actual hours on your first jobs, so you can explain it.

Keep tools on their own line. When the client holds the tracking tool account, they keep the history after the contract ends and the cost split is clear.

Quote setup and the monthly fee separately. Setup is one-off work (fact sheet, schema, llms.txt, FAQ, crawler rules, listings); the monthly fee is changes, measurement and reporting. Spreading setup into the monthly fee loses money when a client leaves after a few months.

Assumptions to write on the quote

AI search quotes usually go wrong on two points: what is included, and what happens if the AI does not mention the client. Adapt the example below to your own terms, and have a lawyer check it before it goes into a contract.

[Assumptions to put on the quote or statement of work — example]
1. Scope: copy, FAQ, structured data, llms.txt and robots.txt on [site URL].
   Pages as listed in Schedule A (X pages). X locations.
2. Not included: new articles, design changes, ads, review requests, listings not
   in Schedule A. Quoted separately if needed.
3. Client inputs: the Client supplies prices, hours, licences and other facts with
   a source. If a review takes longer than X business days, deadlines move by the same.
4. Results: AI answers change by day and by wording. We do not guarantee that an AI
   will show the business or rank it. This quote covers the listed work and
   deliverables (the monthly record and report).
5. Measurement: each month we ask X agreed prompts on X AI engines, X runs each,
   and record the answers and the sources cited.
6. Tools: [tool] is contracted in the Client's name and paid by the Client directly.
7. Changes: added pages, prompts or locations are priced at the unit rates in the
   estimate sheet and started only after written agreement.
8. Term and exit: minimum X months, X days' notice. Settings and records placed on
   the site remain the Client's after the contract ends.

Always keep item 4. AI answers change by day and by wording, and no agency can guarantee a mention. If a client asks for payment on results, agree first which prompts, which engines, how many runs and what counts as an appearance, or you will argue about the measurement. Charging for the work and the record, and reading the trend together, is the safer model.

Item 8 matters too: almost half of the published monthly plans in the Japanese count above stated no minimum term, which is how cancellation disputes start.

If you subcontract the work, or take it from a larger agency, check the payment-term and written-terms rules where you operate. In Japan, for example, the Act on Ensuring Proper Transactions with Small and Medium-sized Contractors took effect on January 1, 2026: when it applies, the commissioning business must state the work, the price, the payment date and the payment method in writing or by email, and pay within 60 days of receipt. Freelancers there are covered by a separate act in force since November 1, 2024.

Sources: Japan Government Public Relations Online and Japan Fair Trade Commission, freelance act (both in Japanese).

Quotes to avoid

  1. Promising results ("guaranteed AI mentions", "top 3 in ChatGPT"). AI answers change and you cannot back it up
  2. A single "AI optimization package" line. Nobody knows what is included, and extra work turns into a dispute
  3. Hiding tool costs in the labour price. The client loses its history when it leaves, and you cannot explain the split
  4. Charging a premium for the AI label alone. If the work is the same as ordinary SEO or site maintenance, price it at the same rate
  5. Leaving out the minimum term and what stays with the client after exit

Checklist before you send the quote

  1. The work is split into audit, setup, monthly upkeep, and measurement and reporting
  2. Each task has a unit (prompts, locations, page templates, listings)
  3. Time per unit comes from your own logs (median of the first three clients)
  4. The hourly rate comes from your fixed costs and billable hours
  5. Tools are on their own line, with the account holder agreed
  6. Contingency is set from the gap between estimated and actual hours
  7. Scope, exclusions, client inputs and change handling are on the quote
  8. The quote says results are not guaranteed and describes how you measure
  9. For subcontracted work, you checked the written-terms and payment rules that apply
  10. The minimum term and what stays with the client after exit are written down

FAQ

Should I just match the market rate for AEO?

Market ranges are selling prices for very different bundles, from tracking only to content and PR. Build your price from your own tasks and hours, and use the ranges only as context when you explain it.

How many hours does AEO setup take?

It depends on how the site is built and how practised your team is, so this guide gives no figure. Log time and quantity per task on your first three clients and use the median time per unit. Most published hour ranges assume consulting or content production.

Can I price AEO on results?

Only if you first agree which prompts, which engines, how many runs and what counts as an appearance, because AI answers change by day and wording. Charging for the work and the record is safer.

Can I drop the setup fee and charge monthly only?

You can, but setup hours spread into the monthly fee lose money if the client leaves early. If you waive setup, state a minimum term on the quote.

Who should pay for the tracking tool?

Ideally the client, in its own name, so it keeps the history after the contract ends. If you pay, list it separately at cost.

Can the second client in the same industry be cheaper?

Often, because exported settings and FAQ templates cut the fact-sheet and FAQ time. Confirm the saving in your logs before you lower the price or add work.

What if a larger agency subcontracts the work to us?

Ask for the work, the price, the payment date and the payment method in writing, check the payment term and how scope changes will be priced, and check the subcontracting rules where you operate. In Japan these are set by a law in force since January 1, 2026.
