AI search work for regulated clients: an agency review process and who is responsible for what

When a client works in a regulated industry (healthcare, dietary supplements, cosmetics, insurance, financial services, law), review the FAQ, structured data and llms.txt you add for AI to the same standard as the rest of the site. The FTC says anyone who takes part in deceptive marketing is potentially liable and has acted against ad agencies, and FINRA requires a principal to approve retail communications before use. Before taking the work, put three things in the contract: the client supplies the facts and their sources, the client's named reviewer approves every change before it goes live, and the agency keeps the approved versions.

Written November 27, 2026 (JST). We read the FTC's Health Products Compliance Guidance, FINRA Rule 2210, FINRA Regulatory Notice 26-14 and the 2026 FINRA oversight report on communications, plus Japan's medical advertising guideline, on the same day.

This guide is for web, SEO and marketing agencies asked to do AI search work for clinics, supplement and cosmetics brands, insurance agencies, financial firms or law firms. It is not legal advice: whether content meets the rules is for the client and its counsel or compliance team to decide. AI answers also change from day to day, and careful review does not guarantee that an AI will mention the client.

The review workflow: six steps and who owns each

The workflows in the top articles name owners such as "compliance" or "legal" but don't say what the agency does and what the client does. Here is the split when an agency does the work.

  1. Classify (agency): list every page and part you will touch, and flag anything that mentions prices, outcomes, risks, comparisons or testimonials. Agree with the client whether unflagged facts (address, hours, directions) can go through a lighter review.
  2. Build a fact sheet (client supplies, agency compiles): prices, what the service or product is, risks and side effects, licences and registration numbers, products carried, each with its source document and date. AI search copy uses only facts on this sheet.
  3. Draft (agency): write page copy, FAQ, structured data and llms.txt descriptions in the fact sheet's wording. If AI wrote the first draft, record that, and have a person check it line by line against the sheet.
  4. Review and approve (client's named reviewer): send the structured data and llms.txt content as well as screenshots. A diff showing what changed makes review faster.
  5. Publish (agency): publish only the approved version, then check that the page, structured data and llms.txt match what was approved.
  6. Record and review (agency keeps records, client sets the review cycle): keep the approval date, approver, pages and published version. Review when prices or rules change, and on the agreed cycle.

What to put in the contract

Write the split of responsibility into the agreement or statement of work, not just an email thread. These are example clauses. Have counsel review them before you use them.

[Clauses to add to the agreement or statement of work — example]
1. Scope: site copy, FAQ, structured data (JSON-LD), llms.txt, image alt text and page
   titles. Parts written only for AI systems are reviewed to the same standard as visible pages.
2. Sources of facts: the Client supplies prices, treatment or product details, risks,
   licences and product lists with a source (price list, approval, policy wording, etc.).
   The Agency does not publish a fact without a source.
3. Review and approval: before publishing, the Agency sends the draft and the diff to the
   Client's named reviewer (name and role in Schedule A). Nothing goes live without approval.
4. Regulatory judgement: the Client makes the final call on whether content meets the rules
   that apply to it. The Agency flags concerns. The Client consults counsel, its compliance
   function, or its regulator or carrier where needed.
5. Generative AI: the Agency tells the Client before using AI to draft. A person checks every
   draft before it is sent. No patient or customer personal data goes into an AI tool.
6. Records: the Agency keeps each approved version (date, approver, pages) for X years and
   provides it on request.
7. Review cycle: affected content is reviewed whenever prices, rules or products change,
   and at least every X months.
8. Regulator or partner findings: if a regulator, carrier or professional body raises an
   issue, the Agency helps unpublish or correct the content within X business days.

The key is the split: the client owns the facts and the final regulatory call, and the agency owns the wording and the records. Since the FTC can act against anyone who takes part in deceptive marketing, "the client approved it" does not fully protect the agency. Keep a record of the concerns you raised as well.

Using generative AI for drafts

  1. Never paste patient or customer names or case details into an AI tool; give it only facts from the fact sheet
  2. AI tends to drop conditions and state things absolutely. Search drafts for "guaranteed", "always", "best", "No. 1", "painless" and similar words
  3. Delete any fact the AI added (statistics, case counts, comparisons) that is not on the fact sheet
  4. Don't let AI write anything that reads like a customer or patient testimonial
  5. Record that AI produced the draft and who checked it

Checking what AI answers say

In regulated industries the real risk is an AI stating a wrong benefit or an old price for the client. Once a month, ask AI assistants the questions customers ask ("what does a dental implant cost in [city]", "which agencies in [city] sell [insurer] policies") and record each answer and its sources. FAQ

Do advertising rules apply to llms.txt and structured data?

None of the regulator documents we read names them. They are public files that name the business and promote its services, so the safe course is to review them to the same standard as the page.

If the client approves everything, is the agency off the hook?

Not necessarily. The FTC says anyone who takes part in deceptive marketing is potentially liable and has brought cases against ad agencies. Keep records of the concerns you raised as well as the approvals.

Can a clinic show Google reviews on its website?

It depends on where the clinic is and which rules apply. Testimonials need the same substantiation as any claim, and some countries, Japan among them, ban patient testimonials in medical advertising. Ask the client's counsel before you embed reviews or add rating markup.

Short FAQ answers lose the conditions. What should we do?

Keep the conditions (who it's for, cost, sessions, risks) in the answer and link to the full page for the rest. AI systems often lift short answers word for word, so avoid unconditional statements.

Can we use AI to draft the FAQ?

Yes, if you give it only facts from the client's fact sheet, a person checks every line, and the client approves before anything is published. Delete any statistic or comparison the AI added that has no source.

Review is slowing down our monthly work.

Agree at the start that facts with no prices or outcomes (address, hours, directions) get a lighter review. Require full approval only for content that covers prices, outcomes, risks, comparisons or testimonials.

An AI is stating a wrong benefit about our client. What now?

Check and fix the client's site and listings first, then record the answer and its sources. AI answers change over time, so a fix may not show up straight away. Re-ask the same questions monthly.
