12 AI search optimization (AEO / GEO) myths: what the official sources and our own data actually say, and how to explain it to clients

Most AI search myths come from three assumptions: that AI needs its own special tactics, that adding one file or tag gets you into AI answers, and that AI answers have rankings you can win. Google says optimizing for its generative AI features is still SEO and needs no AI-only files or markup. What actually helps is keeping the facts AI reads consistent across your site and listings, not blocking AI search crawlers, and checking how you show up by asking fixed questions repeatedly.

Written November 13, 2026. What Google, OpenAI and Perplexity say was checked on their own pages while writing other guides on this site (October 31 to November 12, 2026); each claim links to its source. These pages change, so check the current versions before you rely on them with a client.

This page is for agencies, SEO consultants and local marketing firms who get asked "is it true that...?" by clients. For each myth it gives what is actually true, how strong the evidence is, how to check it yourself, and one sentence you can say to the client. Business owners can use it the same way. AI answers change from one question to the next, and no tactic can guarantee that an AI will mention you or rank you anywhere.

At a glance: 12 myths and how strong the evidence is

Evidence is graded three ways. Official: the company that runs the system says so on its own pages. Our data: something Nurevo measured itself, with conditions. No statement: no official explanation exists, so nobody can say for sure. Passing this distinction on to clients as it is is the most honest way to explain AI search.

Myths 1 and 2: "AI search is separate from SEO" and "SEO no longer matters"

Google says its generative AI features are built on its core Search systems, and that optimizing for them is still SEO (Google: Optimizing for generative AI features, updated July 10, 2026). The same page says a page must be indexed and eligible to be shown with a snippet to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode. So "SEO no longer matters" has it backwards: without the SEO foundation you cannot appear in Google's AI features at all.

Saying "it is exactly SEO, so there is nothing new to do" goes too far the other way. Because AI answers questions directly, it matters more that facts such as hours, prices, service areas and services are stated plainly somewhere on your site and do not contradict your listings. ChatGPT and Perplexity also read pages with their own crawlers, separate from Google's, so checking that those are not blocked is an extra step. The naming differences between AEO, GEO and LLMO are covered in AEO vs SEO vs GEO.

What to tell the client: "The foundation of AI search optimization is the same as SEO. On top of it we make sure the facts AI uses in answers are stated correctly on your site, and that ChatGPT's and Perplexity's crawlers can read it."

Myth 3: "Adding llms.txt gets you into AI answers"

llms.txt is a proposed format for summarizing a site and its main pages for AI (llmstxt.org). Google states that you do not need to create new AI text files for its generative AI features and that Google Search ignores llms.txt (Google: Optimizing for generative AI features). For ChatGPT and Perplexity, we have not found an official page saying they read llms.txt and use it in answers, as of writing.

In our study of 62 local businesses in Japan, measured on Perplexity, the score for on-site work including llms.txt showed no relationship with how often a business was named or cited (see myth 5). For context, when we checked 2,357 Japanese web agency sites in November 2026, 321 (13.6%) had an llms.txt (research report).

Is it pointless, then? The accurate framing is "a harmless, low-effort extra". Having the key points of a site in one place may make it easier for AI agents, or for services that adopt the format later, to read. But no effect can be promised. See how to write llms.txt.

What to tell the client: "llms.txt is a helper file that does no harm, but it will not get you into AI answers on its own. Google says outright that it does not use it."

Myths 4 and 5: "There is AI-only structured data" and "a perfect score gets you in"

Google says no special schema.org markup is needed for its generative AI features (Google: Optimizing for generative AI features), and asks that structured data match what is visible on the page (Google: AI features and your website). A proposal to "add AI-specific markup" easily becomes a product with no basis. What helps is ordinary structured data for the business, such as name, address, phone, hours and business type, published once, without duplicates, and with the same values the page shows.

So does tidying structured data and llms.txt to raise a score make AI more likely to mention you? On October 24, 2026 we compared, for 62 local businesses in Japan, our on-site score (structured data, llms.txt, crawler access and similar) with how often Perplexity named the business or cited its site when asked for recommendations. We found no relationship (Spearman ρ = 0.03, 95% CI −0.23 to 0.28). With 62 businesses only fairly strong relationships can be detected reliably, so a weaker one cannot be ruled out (full study).

Our score checks whether AI can read a site. It does not predict whether AI will pick it. That is our own product's number, and we state the result plainly so it does not mislead clients.

What to tell the client: "Structured data is basic upkeep so AI and search engines get your facts right. Raising a score is not the goal; we tidy things up and then check real AI answers."

Myth 6: "Blocking every AI crawler is the safe choice"

Some AI crawlers collect pages to train models; others read pages to build AI search answers. OpenAI publishes OAI-SearchBot (search) and GPTBot (training) as separate crawlers, and says sites that opt out of OAI-SearchBot will not be shown in ChatGPT search answers (OpenAI: overview of crawlers). Perplexity publishes PerplexityBot and Perplexity-User (Perplexity: crawlers).

A blanket "block all AI bots" rule can therefore close the door to AI search while meaning only to stop training. The reverse also holds: by OpenAI's description, blocking GPTBot while allowing OAI-SearchBot does not remove a site from ChatGPT search answers. Whether to allow training is the client's own decision, separate from AI search.

In our research, of 2,158 Japanese web agency sites with a robots.txt, 28 (1.3%) disallowed the home page for at least one AI bot, and none blocked the search crawlers OAI-SearchBot or Claude-SearchBot (research report). Most blocks targeted training crawlers.

What to tell the client: "Stopping AI training on your content and appearing in AI search are separate choices. Let's allow the search crawlers and decide on training crawlers as you prefer."

Myths 7 and 8: "#1 in AI can be guaranteed" and "one failed question means failure"

On its page about hiring an SEO, Google says no one can guarantee a #1 ranking on Google (Google: Do you need an SEO?). It also advises treating claims of improvement in AI experiences from third-party tools and advice with care (Google: Third-party SEO tools and advice). AI answers change with the day, the run, whether you are signed in and where you are, and they have no fixed ranking like a results page.

For the same reason, a client asking ChatGPT once and not seeing their business tells you little about whether the work succeeded. The method we use is to fix a set of questions that do not include the business name, ask each several times under the same conditions, and record the share of answers that name the business and the share that cite its site, separately. Month-by-month trends make it easier to tell real change from noise (how to measure AI visibility).

What to tell the client: "AI answers change every time, so we don't judge from one answer. We ask fixed questions repeatedly under the same conditions and report the trend."

Myth 9: "Tools can show how AI rates you internally"

Google says no third-party tool has access to its internal ranking or AI systems (Google: Optimizing for generative AI features). What AI visibility tools do is send fixed questions to AI services and count whether the answers include a name or a source. That is a useful sample, not a score from the AI.

When choosing a tool, check that it states which AI services it measures, how many questions and runs it uses, whether questions include the business name, and how failed runs are handled. Differences between tools are covered in AEO tools compared.

What to tell the client: "A tool shows a sample of real answers to fixed questions. Nobody can see how the AI rates you inside."

Myth 10: "Fixes show up at once" and "the AI company will fix it for you"

We found no official statement of how long it takes AI services or search engines to re-read a corrected page and reflect it in answers. Nor could we find, on any of the companies' official pages, a way for a business to have an AI answer rewritten on request. Google's AI Overviews let users report a problem with an answer, but that is not a reliable way to correct it (Google Search Help: AI Overviews).

The work that matters is fixing the information AI reads: your own site, your Google Business Profile and the sites that list you. Record the date of each fix, then re-ask the same questions at a set interval, such as every one or two weeks.

What to tell the client: "There is no way to edit an AI answer directly. We fix the information AI reads, record the date, and check with the same questions."

Myths 11 and 12: "Search Console shows AI clicks" and "a local business only needs its site"

The Generative AI performance report in Search Console shows how many times links to your site were shown in AI Overviews and AI Mode. It shows impressions; clicks and positions from AI features are not available in that report (Search Console Help: Generative AI performance report). ChatGPT and Perplexity are outside Search Console, so combine it with fixed-question measurement and referrers in your analytics (how to see AI features in Search Console).

For local businesses, Google recommends keeping Business Profile information up to date (Google: AI features and your website). If the hours on the site and on the Business Profile differ, there is no telling which one an AI will give. Stating the same facts the same way across the site, structured data, Business Profile and main listings is the work with the best expected payoff for a local business (adding business schema with SEO plugins).

What to tell the client: "Search Console only shows impressions in Google's AI features. The first job is making your site and Business Profile say the same thing."

How to check it yourself (on WordPress)

Myths fall away faster when you check the client's site together than when you explain. This takes about 30 minutes, in this order.

  1. Open yoursite/robots.txt in a browser and check that search crawlers such as OAI-SearchBot and PerplexityBot are not disallowed (myth 6). On WordPress, SEO and security plugins sometimes rewrite robots.txt, so check their settings too.
  2. Put the home page and About page URLs into the Rich Results Test. Check that business structured data is present and that hours and address match the page (myth 4), and that the SEO plugin and the theme are not both outputting it.
  3. Open yoursite/llms.txt and check whether it exists and matches current services and prices. If there is none you can add one, but promise no effect (myth 3).
  4. Compare hours, phone and address on the Google Business Profile with the site and align them to the correct values (myth 12).
  5. With the client, choose 3 to 5 questions that do not include the business name, ask them in ChatGPT, Perplexity and Google's AI Mode, and record the date and conditions, whether the business was named and whether the site was cited (myths 7 and 8).
  6. If you have Search Console access, check impressions in the Generative AI performance report and make sure the Search generative AI setting is not excluding the site by mistake (myth 11).

Building a one-page explainer for clients

The points above fit on one page, which works well for getting on the same page with a client. A suggested outline follows. The trick is to give "what we can do" as much space as the myths.

  1. What AI search optimization is (one or two sentences): SEO as the base, plus stating the facts AI uses correctly and checking how you show up.
  2. What we can do: check crawler access, align facts across structured data, the site and the Business Profile, set up helpers such as llms.txt, and measure and report on fixed questions regularly.
  3. What we cannot do: guarantee appearance or a position, edit AI answers directly, see how AI rates you inside, or promise how long changes take.
  4. Common myths (3 to 5): pick the ones this client worries about from the table above, each with a link to the official page.
  5. How we check: which questions, on which AI services, how many runs, how often, and what we report.

FAQ

Are LLMO and GEO meaningless terms?

They are names for nearly the same goal: preparing the information AI reads so a business is represented correctly in AI answers. Deciding which work to do and how to check it matters more than the label.

Should I avoid adding llms.txt?

Adding one is fine. But Google says Search ignores it, and we have found no official statement that other AI services use it, so treat it as a helper and promise no effect.

If I block GPTBot, will I disappear from ChatGPT?

OpenAI treats OAI-SearchBot (search) and GPTBot (training) as separate crawlers, and says it is opting out of OAI-SearchBot that keeps a site out of ChatGPT search answers. Check which one your robots.txt names before blocking.

A client wants to be "#1 in AI". What do I say?

Explain that AI answers have no fixed ranking and change even for the same question, and that Google itself says no one can guarantee #1. Then offer to measure the share of fixed questions that name the business and report the trend.

Can I check these myths myself?

Yes. robots.txt, structured data, llms.txt and the Business Profile can each be opened and checked directly, and how you show up in AI can be checked by asking questions without your name repeatedly under the same conditions. See "How to check it yourself" above.

How long will this page stay accurate?

AI systems and the companies' documentation keep changing. This page reflects checks as of November 13, 2026. Before explaining to a client, confirm the current wording on the linked official pages.
