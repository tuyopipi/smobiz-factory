AI search for WordPress maintenance providers: a checklist so routine work does not stop AI reading your clients' sites

For a WordPress maintenance provider, the first job in AI search optimization is not adding new tactics but checking that routine maintenance (migrations, security and CDN settings, plugin and theme changes, robots.txt edits) has not broken AI crawler access or structured data. Split the checks into three moments, after each job, every month and at migration, and they fit into the maintenance process you already run.

Written November 10, 2026 (JST). What Cloudflare and WordPress say was checked in their own documentation that day. Their behaviour can change, so check the current pages before you work.

This guide is for companies and freelancers who already maintain WordPress sites. How to design AI search as a monthly service (scope and pricing) is covered in How agencies can offer AI search optimization monthly. Here we focus on the accidents that quietly make a site invisible to AI during everyday maintenance, and how to catch them.

Why maintenance providers hold the key to AI search

When ChatGPT, Perplexity or Google's AI features answer a question about a business, they work from the pages their crawlers could read and the facts on them. Google says there are no additional requirements or special optimizations needed to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode, and that SEO best practices still apply (Google Search Central: AI features and your website). OpenAI says sites that disallow OAI-SearchBot will not be shown in ChatGPT search answers (OpenAI: overview of crawlers).

So whether AI can read a site comes down to whether crawlers can get in, whether indexing is refused, and whether the structured data and page text state the right facts. Every one of those can change during maintenance. A setting left over from a migration, bot protection added while hardening security, a switch of SEO plugin: well-meant work can stop AI from seeing a site.

These accidents are invisible to a person looking at the site. It looks normal; enquiries just dip a little. That is why the people who touch the site every month are best placed to own the checks. AI answers also vary between days and runs of the same question: these checks keep a site readable for AI, they do not guarantee that an AI will mention it.

The "do not index" setting that survives migrations

Since WordPress 5.3, "Discourage search engines from indexing this site" no longer adds Disallow to robots.txt; it outputs a robots meta tag (noindex, nofollow) on the pages instead (Make WordPress Core: changes to prevent search engines indexing sites). If you only look at robots.txt and see nothing wrong, you will miss it. Check for meta name='robots' in the HTML source.

The same post recommends that development and staging sites send an X-Robots-Tag: noindex, nofollow header from the server, covering images and PDFs too. That is a server or hosting setting, not a WordPress one, so after switching to production, check the response headers to make sure it has not been left on.

Are security, CDN or WAF settings blocking AI crawlers?

This is the hardest one to notice when a maintenance provider tightens security. Many bot protections can lump AI crawlers in with "unknown automated traffic" and stop them all.

Cloudflare, for example, has a setting that groups AI crawlers and agents by behaviour: Search (collecting or indexing content to answer questions later), Agent (acting in real time on a person's behalf) and Training (using content to train models). For each you choose Block, Block on pages with ads, or Allow. Its documentation says new domains use Allow for all three by default, and that Training also covers mixed-purpose crawlers used for both training and search (Cloudflare: AI Crawler and Agent access policies, updated November 2, 2026). If you change the setting to stop training, read what it will stop before applying it.

The server access log is the most reliable check. Are requests from OAI-SearchBot, PerplexityBot and similar arriving, and do they get 200 or 403? You can also test with curl by sending only a crawler's user agent, but Cloudflare says it identifies verified bots by a cryptographic signature, a published IP list or reverse DNS (Cloudflare: Verified bots), so a request that merely claims to be a crawler may not be treated like the real one. Do not rely on that test alone.

Crawler names and purposes are documented by each company (OpenAI, Perplexity). Whether to allow training crawlers such as GPTBot is a separate decision from appearing in AI search, so let the client make it rather than deciding for them.

Maintenance checklist: after each job, monthly, at migration

Written so you can paste it into your existing maintenance runbook.

  1. [After each job] After plugin or theme updates and setting changes, check the HTML source of the home page and key pages (services, pricing, location, FAQ): no noindex, and application/ld+json describing one business.
  2. [After each job] When you change security, CDN or WAF settings, log what and when, and check the status codes AI search crawlers get in the access log over the following days.
  3. [Monthly] Check that /robots.txt and /llms.txt return 200 with the content you intend. How to write llms.txt
  4. [Monthly] Ask the client whether hours, prices, address or services changed, and compare the page, structured data, llms.txt and external profiles.
  5. [Monthly] Ask AI your fixed set of questions and record whether the business was named and the site cited (optional; see How to measure AI visibility).
  6. [At migration] Make sure "Discourage search engines", X-Robots-Tag headers, Basic auth and IP restrictions have not reached production.
  7. [At migration] Check whether a robots.txt file came across to the server and, if so, that its content is what you intend.
  8. [When changing SEO plugin] Structured data output changes, so check for duplicates and gaps. Adding business schema to your SEO plugin

What to tell clients and put in the contract

  1. Do not promise appearances or positions in AI. Promise how often you check, what you check and what you do when you find something.
  2. Record the client's decision on training crawlers, and explain it separately from AI search crawlers.
  3. Tell clients before installing plugins or changing robots.txt. A sample notice is on the partner program page.
  4. When the CDN or WAF account is in the client's name, agree who may change its settings.

FAQ

Where should a maintenance provider start with AI search?

Add two things to your current runbook: a check after each job (no noindex, structured data present) and a monthly check of robots.txt and llms.txt. Keeping the site readable for AI comes before any new tactic.

Does tightening security make a site less likely to appear in AI search?

It depends on the settings. If bot protection or a WAF also stops AI search crawlers, those AIs cannot read the pages. Some services let you allow or block by purpose, so check what a setting stops before applying it, then confirm the status codes in the access log.

If robots.txt looks fine, is the site definitely not refusing indexing?

No. Since WordPress 5.3, "Discourage search engines" works through a robots meta tag, not robots.txt, and a server can also refuse indexing with an X-Robots-Tag header. Check both the HTML source and the response headers.

Is it fine to block AI training crawlers?

That is the client's call. Training crawlers such as GPTBot are a separate decision from AI search crawlers such as OAI-SearchBot. Let the client choose, and record what they chose.

Do we need to measure AI visibility every month?

Not necessarily. The minimum for maintenance is checking that AI can read the site. Measuring visibility can be added for clients who want it, by asking a fixed set of questions under the same conditions.
