AI search for monument masons and Buddhist altar shops: getting AI to read your headstone prices, cemeteries, removals and product origin correctly

AI search optimization (AEO) for a headstone or monument business, or a shop selling Buddhist home altars, comes down to writing, as text on your own site, what people want to know before they ask for a quote. For memorials: the tax-inclusive total split into stone, lettering, foundation and installation, what is not included, the cemeteries you work in, and exactly which parts of a removal or reinstatement you do. For altars: size, materials, where it was made, delivery and taking back an old one. Then publish the same facts in your Google Business Profile and in structured data (Store with Service).

Written November 26, 2026 (JST). Several examples here come from Japan, where Buddhist altar shops and stonemasons are often the same business and where we checked the public records on November 26, 2026. Cemetery, permit and consumer rules differ by country and by cemetery, so check the ones that apply to you. This article is not legal advice.

AI answers change from day to day and with how a question is phrased. The steps here make your facts readable to AI. They do not guarantee that an AI will mention you or rank you anywhere.

What people ask AI about memorials and altars

Most people buy a headstone or a home altar once or twice in their lives, often soon after a death. So the questions they put to AI are basic and practical: how much will it really cost, what exactly will you do, and can you work in the cemetery where we already have a plot. Plots, burial rights and cemetery fees are for the cemetery to answer; this article covers what the monument mason or altar shop should answer.

Write memorial prices as an itemised total, with exclusions

If only the stone price is prominent, both customers and AI get the total wrong, because a memorial also involves cutting, lettering, a foundation, fixing and sometimes removing an old stone. Even if you sell packages, say in words what is in and what is out.

Amounts are placeholders. Write your real prices and the real names of your stones.
ItemExample wording
StoneUpright headstone in [named granite] from [country], $X; in [named stone] from [country], $Y (including tax)
LetteringUp to 60 letters included; additional letters $Z each
FoundationConcrete foundation for a standard lawn plot included
Fixing and deliveryIncluded for cemeteries within our area; outside it, quoted by distance before you commit
Not includedCemetery permit or installation fees charged by the cemetery, plot purchase, removal of an old memorial
Total exampleUpright headstone with 60 letters on a lawn plot in [town]: $X (sum of the items above)

In the United States, the FTC says its Funeral Rule does not apply to third-party sellers such as monument dealers, or to cemeteries that lack an on-site funeral home, so price-list rules written for funeral homes may not cover you. FTC: The FTC Funeral Rule. Writing an itemised price anyway answers the question customers actually ask. For pricing pages in general, see pricing pages and AI search.

Name the cemeteries you work in

Families usually already have a plot, so the first practical question is whether you can work there. The FTC's guide to buying a cemetery site tells consumers to check the type of monuments or memorials a cemetery allows. FTC: Buying a Cemetery Site. Cemetery rules on size, material and who may install vary, so your site should say where you have worked and what you handle.

  1. List the cemeteries, churchyards or temple graveyards you have worked in, grouped by town. Ask private or religious grounds before naming them.
  2. If a cemetery approves or registers masons and you are approved there, say so. Never claim an approval you do not hold.
  3. Say whether you apply for the cemetery's permit on the customer's behalf, and how any fee the cemetery charges is passed on.
  4. Name the towns you cover, and how you price work in cemeteries further away.

Removals and moves: split your work from other people's steps

In Japan, closing a family grave and moving the remains (改葬) has become far more common: a regional office of the Ministry of Internal Affairs reports that relocation permits rose from about 84,000 in fiscal 2014 to about 176,000 in fiscal 2024, based on the health ministry's administrative statistics. MIC Tohoku Regional Bureau (PDF, Japanese). Japan's National Consumer Affairs Center recorded 1,046, 1,018 and 1,077 consultations about graves in fiscal 2023, 2024 and 2025 (as of May 31, 2026); its recent examples involve temples asking for large fees when a family leaves. National Consumer Affairs Center (Japanese)

A mason does not set those fees, but the lesson travels: customers struggle to see who they pay, for what. Whatever the local process, write it as two lists.

Buddhist altars: size, materials and origin, exactly as they are

For a home altar, write the height, width and depth, the materials (wood, solid or veneer, lacquer finish), where it was made and the price. Origin matters most when customers compare. In Japan, 241 crafts are designated as traditional crafts by the Minister of Economy, Trade and Industry (as of October 27, 2025), including several regional altar traditions such as Hikone. Shiga Prefecture (Japanese). Osaka altars were designated on November 1, 1982, Mikawa altars on December 15, 1976 and Hiroshima altars in 1978. Osaka · Aichi · Hiroshima

Designated crafts can carry a certification label; Shiga Prefecture explains that a product with the label has passed an inspection carried out by the production-area cooperative or a similar body. Shiga Prefecture · Association for the Promotion of Traditional Craft Industries (PDF, Japanese). "Nagoya Butsudan" is also a registered regional collective trademark (No. 5098956). Chubu Bureau of Economy, Trade and Industry (PDF, Japanese)

  1. Use a regional name only for altars made there and backed by something checkable, such as the certification label. Avoid wording like "[region] style" that reads as a claim of origin.
  2. If an altar was made abroad, or made abroad and finished locally, say which steps happened where. AI can then answer both "made in Japan" and "best value" questions accurately.
  3. For compact modern altars, give dimensions and placement examples (on a shelf, on a cabinet X cm wide).
  4. State delivery, set-up and take-back charges. If customers usually consult their temple before an old altar is removed, say what you do and what they arrange themselves.

Inscriptions, repairs and guarantees: price and lead time

  1. Additional inscriptions: price per name or per letter, whether carved in the cemetery or in the workshop, and how long it takes.
  2. Cleaning and re-fixing: an example price per memorial, and whether you send before-and-after photos.
  3. Guarantee: how many years and what it covers (cracks, joints, settling), and what happens after storms or earthquakes. Conditions are more useful to AI than "lifetime guarantee".
  4. Altar restoration: typical prices for cleaning, re-lacquering or re-gilding, and how you quote (photos, in store or a home visit).

Keep showroom details identical on your site and Google Business Profile

Many monument and altar businesses have a showroom or display yard, so keep the address, hours, closing days, parking and what is on display the same on your site and your Google Business Profile. Google's guidelines do not allow adding places or service keywords to the business name. Google: Guidelines for representing your business. Publish holiday and seasonal hours on both as soon as they are set; see business hours and holidays.

  1. If you sell memorials and altars, keep one set of business details and give each its own page.
  2. With several showrooms, give each one a page. Multi-location businesses
  3. If you sell altars or fittings online, put shipping and returns on each product page. Online shops and AI search

Structured data: no memorial or altar type, so use Store with Service

schema.org has no dedicated type for monument masons or altar shops (checked in schema-dts 2.1.0 on November 26, 2026). Cemetery describes the burial ground as a place, not a business. Publish the business as Store (or LocalBusiness), describe each job, such as a new memorial, an added inscription, a removal or an altar restoration, as a Service with an Offer, and put your area in areaServed. Store has subtypes such as HomeGoodsStore and FurnitureStore, but none fits an altar shop closely; Store is enough.

Keep structured data identical to the prices on the page, and never put money you do not receive, such as cemetery fees or religious offerings, into an Offer price.

Pre-publish checklist

  1. Memorial prices are split into stone, lettering, foundation and fixing, including tax, with a total example.
  2. Money paid to the cemetery is separated from money paid to you.
  3. Stones are named, with their country or quarry of origin.
  4. Cemeteries you work in, and any where you are approved, are listed by name and kept separate.
  5. Removal work you do is separated from permits and steps you do not handle.
  6. Altar dimensions, materials and origin are accurate, and regional or designated-craft names are used only with evidence.
  7. Delivery, take-back, inscription and repair prices and lead times are written.
  8. Guarantee length and coverage are written with conditions.
  9. Address, hours and seasonal hours match your Google Business Profile.
  10. Structured data is Store (or LocalBusiness) with Service, prices match the page, and llms.txt lists the main pages. What is llms.txt

Check whether AI mentions you, and whether it is right

Once your pages are in order, ask AI what customers would ask. Check whether you are named for questions such as "headstone removal in [town]", "masons who work in [cemetery]" or "small Buddhist altar shop in [town]", and ask with your name, such as "What does an extra inscription cost?" or "Do they take old altars?", to see whether the answers match your site. Watch for cemetery fees being presented as your price, and for services you no longer offer.

FAQ

Where should a monument mason start with AI search?

First, write memorial prices as a tax-inclusive total split into stone, lettering, foundation and fixing, and separate them from cemetery fees. Then list the cemeteries you work in and what you do and do not handle for removals. After that, match your Google Business Profile and publish the same facts in structured data and llms.txt.

Will publishing headstone prices hurt me?

There is no official information that publishing prices makes AI less likely to mention a business. An itemised total with an example lets AI answer what is included and at what cost. Where prices depend on plot or stone, give the conditions and an example.

Should I explain permits for removing or moving a grave?

Separate the work you do from the permits and steps others handle, and link to the official guidance for the details, because the rules differ by place and cemetery.

Which structured data type fits a monument or altar shop?

schema.org has no dedicated type, so use Store or LocalBusiness and describe each job as a Service. Cemetery is a type for the burial ground itself, not for a shop.

Will saying an altar was made abroad cost me sales?

There is no official information that stating origin makes AI less likely to mention you. Saying which steps happened where lets AI answer both buyers looking for domestic craftsmanship and buyers looking for value. Avoid wording that suggests a regional or designated origin you cannot back up.

Will this guarantee ChatGPT recommends me?

No. AI answers change, and no tool or technique can guarantee a mention or a position. What you can do is make your prices, cemeteries, removal work and product facts easy for AI to read, and keep checking whether you appear and whether the answers are correct.
