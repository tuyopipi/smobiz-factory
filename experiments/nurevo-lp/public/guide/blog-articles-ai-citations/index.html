How to write blog posts that AI search cites: steps checked against official sources, and how to spot "AI writing tricks" with no evidence behind them

For an AI to cite your article, the article first has to be readable by search engines, and the answer has to sit in the text in a form that still makes sense when lifted out on its own. Google says there are no extra requirements and no special optimization for appearing in AI Overviews or AI Mode. What actually makes a difference is three things: answering one question per article, right at the top; adding facts only you have (your numbers, steps and cases); and showing the dates, the author and your sources on the page.

Written 17 November 2026 (JST). Google's Search Central blog post "Top ways to ensure your content performs well in Google's AI experiences on Search" (21 May 2025) was read that day. Google's "AI features and your website", the Search Console generative AI performance report and OpenAI's and Perplexity's crawler documentation are used only as far as other guides on this site confirmed them in October and November 2026. This guide is for people who write company or shop blogs, columns and how-to articles, and for agencies that write them for clients.

AI answers change from day to day and with the wording of the question. These steps make your articles readable and usable by AI; they do not guarantee that you will appear in, or be cited by, any AI answer.

How an article becomes an AI source

ChatGPT search, Perplexity and Google's AI Overviews take a question, search the web, read the pages they find, build an answer and show the pages they used as sources. To become one of those sources, an article first has to be findable in search and readable. Writing technique comes after that.

Before you write: pick one question per article

The questions people ask AI go beyond definitions such as "what is X?". They ask "how do I choose?", "how much does it cost?", "where do I start?" and "what if it doesn't work?". Packing several questions into one article blurs which question each part answers. Decide on one question per article and use it, as asked or lightly tidied, in the title and the first heading.

Your own records are a better source of questions than outside tools. Write down a month of questions from your contact form, email and phone calls, long phrase-like queries in Search Console, and whatever your sales or front-desk staff explain every week. That gives you the list of articles worth writing.

As the last row shows, questions with a place or opening hours in them tend to be answered from facts about the business itself (the About page, location pages, the Google Business Profile) rather than from articles. Do not try to cover them with a blog post; get the business facts right first, for example with local business structured data.

How to write: paragraphs that make sense when lifted out

AI builds its answer from the paragraphs and tables that match the question, not from the whole article. A good test is whether a paragraph still makes sense when read on its own.

  1. Answer the question in the first two or three sentences. Do not open with scene-setting ("In recent years, X has attracted attention") or an introduction of yourself.
  2. Write headings in the words readers use, so the heading tells them what follows ("Typical cost, and what changes it").
  3. Do not lean on "this", "the above" or "as mentioned"; restate the subject inside each paragraph.
  4. Give every number a date and a source ("as of October 2026", "from the X survey"). For your own figures, say how you counted.
  5. Put comparisons in tables, and make them HTML tables. Tables saved as images are hard for both AI and search engines to read.
  6. When the answer depends on circumstances, split it by circumstance ("if you are a sole trader", "if you are a company").
  7. Include facts only you have: real counts, how many days something took, the steps you followed, the usual mistakes and how you fixed them.

Here is a rewrite (a column by a fictional accounting firm).

Before: "In recent years, interest in Making Tax Digital has grown. Our firm has received many enquiries. In this article, we would like to explain the topic in detail in light of the above background."

After: "If you are a sole trader whose income crosses the threshold, you will need software that keeps digital records and sends quarterly updates. Of the clients we helped switch in 2026, most chose the software their bookkeeper already used. These are the three things to decide first."

The rewritten paragraph puts the answer (do I need to act?), the basis for the decision and a first-hand fact (what clients chose) in its first three sentences. Before you state thresholds or rules, check the current figures on the official source (in this case the tax authority) and link to it.

Show the dates, the author and your sources

Google encourages accurate authorship information, such as bylines, wherever readers would expect it, and suggests telling readers how content was produced, including whether automation or AI was used (Google: creating helpful content). Put these four things in the article itself.

  1. Written and updated dates, at the top or bottom. Do not change the updated date for a typo fix. When you review the content, change the date and add one line on what changed; that tells readers and AI how current it is.
  2. The author: name and role, linked to an author profile page (how to build a profile page). If someone reviewed the article, say what they reviewed and when, separately from the author.
  3. Sources: link numbers, rules and other companies' specifications to the primary source (government, the maker's official page, the original study). Avoid quoting a round-up that quotes someone else.
  4. How it was made: if AI drafted it, say so, and say that a person checked it.

Structured data (BlogPosting or Article) publishes the same values in a machine-readable form. Google asks that the content of your markup also be visible on the page (Google Search Central blog). Do not put an author or a date in the markup that the text does not show.

Checklist before you publish

  1. The article answers one question, and that question is in the title and the first heading.
  2. The first two or three sentences answer it.
  3. Each paragraph names its subject and makes sense on its own.
  4. Comparisons, prices and steps are HTML tables or numbered lists, not only images.
  5. Numbers carry a date and a link to their source; numbers you could not check are gone.
  6. Facts only you have (counts, cases, steps, mistakes) are included.
  7. Written date, updated date and author name are visible, and the name links to an author page.
  8. Key answers are not hidden behind tabs or buttons (the text is in the page source).
  9. robots.txt does not block OAI-SearchBot or PerplexityBot; there is no noindex or small max-snippet.
  10. The author, dates and image in the structured data match the text.
  11. No older article answers the same question (if one did, it was merged and redirected).

FAQ

How long should an article be?

There is no fixed length. Write as much as it takes to answer the question fully. Vendors sometimes publish the median length of cited articles, but there is no evidence that adding words helps in itself.

Should every heading be a question?

Not necessarily. What matters is that headings use readers' words and say what follows. "Typical cost, and what changes it" works just as well.

Are AI-written articles never cited?

No official statement says AI use alone rules an article out. But AI drafts tend to contain errors and to say what everyone else says, so have a person check the facts and add your own information before publishing. Google suggests telling readers how content was produced.

Should I delete old articles?

If a newer article answers the same question, move the useful content into it and redirect the old one. If an old article is worth keeping as a record, say at the top which year it describes and link to the newer article.

Does adding an FAQ at the end make citations more likely?

If the questions are ones readers really ask, it helps readers. There is no official statement that it increases citations. Keep the FAQ visible on the page and, if you publish markup, keep it identical to what is shown.

If I refresh the updated date, will AI treat the article as new?

Changing only the date is not recommended. Change it when you review the content and add a line on what changed. A fresh date on stale content costs readers' trust.

If I follow these steps, will AI always cite my article?

That cannot be guaranteed. AI answers vary by day and wording and depend on other sites too. These steps make your article readable and usable by AI.
